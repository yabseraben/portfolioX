Blockchain Carbon Markets and the Ethics of Engineered Sustainability Claims

Research Paper • 2024

Blockchain technology's secure, verifiable design seemed well-suited to environmental markets, where traceability and trust are essential for verifying climate impact [4], [5]. In recent years, blockchain-based carbon credit initiatives like Toucan Protocol, KlimaDAO, and Flowcarbon have made claims of carbon-neutrality and transparency in global carbon trading [1], [3], [15].

Yet independent analysis shows these platforms have issued tokens backed by low-quality, aged, or already retired carbon credits, overstating their environmental value [12]–[14]. The deployment of unverifiable inputs and energy-consuming blockchain protocols undermines their sustainability claims [4], [19]. These systems represent a form of technological greenwashing where discourse about sustainability signals innovation without delivering quantifiable change [6].

How Blockchain Carbon Markets Work

Blockchain is a digital decentralized ledger that records and verifies transactions across a network of computers [5]. After verification, every transaction is slotted into a secured chain of data blocks [7]. This leads to an immutable and verifiable history of transactions that cannot be altered without network approval [7].

In the context of carbon markets, blockchain facilitates the digital issuance, transfer, and verification of carbon credits [3]. Carbon credits are certificates stating that one metric ton of carbon dioxide equivalent (tCO₂e) has been reduced by a specific project [9]. Such projects can include reforestation programs or renewable-energy installations that capture measurable emissions reductions [9]. Upon verification, an equivalent number of credits is created and recorded on the blockchain as digital tokens [3].

Each token holds unique data, including the origin of the project, geographical coordinates, and date of validation, so every credit is unique and traceable [3]. Companies seeking to offset their emissions purchase tokens from the blockchain marketplace and store them in digital wallets as a tokenized asset [2]. Every transaction is permanently recorded on the blockchain, producing an open and time-stamped chain of custody [3].

When an organization "retires" its tokens, meaning offsets have been claimed to cancel out emissions, the corresponding transaction is recorded immutably to the ledger, preventing that credit from being resold or reused [14]. In principle, this creates an auditable, self-contained infrastructure where all stages of a credit's life cycle can be confirmed through a public, decentralized record [5].

Blockchain's Appeal to Carbon Markets

The promise of blockchain-based carbon credit platforms was to solve persistent inefficiencies and credibility issues in traditional carbon markets [4], [5]. Offsets in traditional registries typically have their own verification procedures and proprietary databases with limited public transparency [4]. As a result, it is challenging for buyers to confirm whether a credit has already been sold or retired [4]. This creates risks of double-counting and inconsistent recordkeeping across platforms [9]. Transactions are typically intermediated by administrators and brokers, adding cost and delay to an otherwise straightforward accounting process [4].

Blockchain technology appeared to offer a structural solution to these limitations [4], [5]. By maintaining a shared, tamper-proof ledger, it had the potential to consolidate data into one verifiable record that all parties could share [7]. Its automated smart-contract functionality promised to simplify settlements, reduce administrative overhead, and provide real-time transparency in credit trading [5]. At its core, integrating carbon accounting into a blockchain system would transform traditional, fragmented carbon marketplaces into a structured and auditable system [4].

The Problem of Low-Quality Credits

However, transaction visibility is not synonymous with environmental integrity [6]. The resulting gap between recorded activity and verified climate impact creates conditions in which these systems facilitate greenwashing [6], [8], [12]. One of the fundamental integrity breakdowns in blockchain carbon markets is the on-chain revival of low-quality or previously retired credits, which inflated "climate-positive" claims with minimal climate value [12]–[14]. When tokenized credits were accumulated into large pools, the particular details that indicate a project's quality—such as when it was produced, how it calculated emissions, or cut carbon—became blurred [12]. Some of these credits, including HFC-23 destruction units, have previously been criticized as being among "the most noxious" instances of non-additional crediting [12]. This blending caused older or low-quality credits to appear equivalent to newer, higher-quality ones, concealing differences that are essential in determining actual climate impact [12].

By producing tokens "based on retired credits," projects re-inputted already spent claims and allowed for double-counting, directly disrupting the validity of "carbon-negative" or "climate-positive" marketing [14]. Verra, the largest registry, explicitly prohibited tokenization of retired credits in May 2022 on these grounds [14]. They conceded that this practice misleads buyers and exaggerates climate effectiveness [14]. That ban establishes the deceptive potential at the heart of this practice [14], [15].

In essence, blockchain networks that monitor invalid or already retired carbon credits create the illusion of climate responsibility without effective climate results [12], [14]. Transparency of the ledger then operates as a marketing tool instead of an indicator of integrity [6]. It visually guarantees transactions and not the reduction of emissions [6]. This distinction is what makes the practice a form of greenwashing [6].

The environmental claim is dependent on public visibility of the token exchange [6]. Companies and platforms use the aesthetic of technological precision to imply environmental rigor [6]. This, however, conceals the fact that the credits no longer represent genuine emission reductions [12], [13]. This misuse of transparency turns a technical feature of blockchain into an ethical flaw where clarity in records serves as a proxy for integrity in reporting results [8]. The harm has tangible environmental and ethical consequences [16], [17].

Investors and companies are led to believe that their investments lead to real decarbonization, as funds continue to flow into failing projects [16]. That loss of confidence de-legitimizes carbon markets overall [15]. For engineers, the lesson is clear: when system design is constructed to optimize technological novelty and appearance at the expense of the precision of its environmental measures, it becomes a means of technological greenwashing [8]. These practices breach engineers' professional duties of truthfulness, accuracy, and public interest by confusing the process of recording a transaction with the achievement of a positive climate effect [10], [11].

The problems surrounding low-quality or already-retired credits are compounded by a second weakness in blockchain carbon markets: opaque verification [4]. Even if the credits entering a system appear legitimate, the process used to verify their climate impact tends to rely upon unverifiable sources [4], [6]. On blockchain platforms, this verification comes through oracles, which are the methods that bring off-chain data, such as carbon reduction levels, onto the blockchain [4]. Nonetheless, the bulk of the oracles used in carbon markets are third-party intermediaries with closed systems and data collection/validation processes that are not publicly disclosed [4]. Buyers can see the record on ledgers of transactions, but do not have access to inspect the scientific accuracy of measurements being placed on record [4]. Thus, blockchain provides transparency into ownership and transfers, but not necessarily into a verified positive climate impact [4], [6].

Verification Gaps in Tokenized Systems

This verification gap was salient in the Toucan Protocol, one of the largest blockchain carbon-credit platforms [12]. Toucan's bridge connected Verra's carbon registry to the public blockchain Polygon, which allowed off-chain credits to be transferred to digital tokens [12]. The bridge was founded on registry data transmitted using automated oracle scripts that copied Verra's records onto the blockchain [12]. But the integrity and quality of such inputs were largely taken on trust [12], [14]. This means there were no independent audit mechanisms between on-chain tokens and registry data [14]. Verra later conducted an independent audit and actually found that many of the credits being tokenized under Toucan were aged, low-quality, or even retired credits [14]. The blockchain faithfully tracked every transaction in those tokens, but stored unverified or invalid data [12], [14]. That illustrates techno-greenwashing as the appearance of systemic precision used to market unreliable assertions about climate as scientifically valid [6], [8].

By promoting blockchain transparency as proof of trustworthiness, companies like Toucan obscure the separation between data recording and verification of fact [12]. Companies that purchased these tokens believed they were engaging in actual climate efforts since each transaction and retirement was visible on-chain [12]. In reality, that transparency actually masked the lack of independent validation [12]. A system designed to demonstrate accountability instead becomes a tool for concealing uncertainty [6]. Engineers and developers who design these systems risk misleading the public and misallocating resources from genuine climate mitigation [8]. For sustainable engineering, it is important to prioritize verifiability over visibility [8]. Without rigorous, verifiable pipelines of data, transparency will not enable ethical or environmental integrity [8].

Infrastructure Energy Costs

Another ethical contradiction is the energy use of the technology itself [4], [19]. Though some blockchain carbon platforms are marketed as climate-mitigation technologies, their supporting infrastructure can emit significant carbon, clashing against their environmental claims [19]. Most blockchains consume copious amounts of energy to power to maintain their ledgers [18]. On proof-of-work (PoW) networks like Bitcoin and Ethereum utilize computational power to validate transactions [18]. KlimaDAO operates on the Polygon PoS chain, which advertises itself as low-energy [1]. However, a third-party Offsetra–Polygon–KlimaDAO analysis established that between February 2021 and February 2022, Polygon's activity alone contributed around 85 000 t CO₂e—about the annual electricity usage of 16 000 U.S. homes [19], [20]. Yet KlimaDAO promoted itself as "carbon negative" during this period while its infrastructure relied upon this energy-intensive architecture [1], [13].

MIT Technology Review and CarbonPlan analysts argued that this dynamic "used high-energy blockchain infrastructure to track low-impact carbon credits," illustrating how technological optimism obscured actual emission calculations [12], [13]. This energy paradox is greenwashing because it makes investment in carbon markets appear to be environmentally friendly without accounting for the emissions of technology supporting those transactions [6], [8]. As a result, a company buying or retiring carbon tokens can appear eco-friendly while subsidizing networks that emit far more emissions than they mitigate [4], [19]. Unless the entire energy usage of the network is accounted for, "carbon-negative" promotion is deceptive [19]. For sustainable practice in engineering, environmental accounting must be extended to the infrastructure itself; otherwise, the blockchain systems will contribute to the systems it is meant to reduce [8].

Engineering Accountability and Systemic Pressures

A guiding pillar in engineering ethics is the duty to support public welfare by being accurate, honest, and transparent [10], [11]. The National Society of Professional Engineers (NSPE) Code of Ethics guides engineers to "hold paramount the safety, health, and welfare of the public" and make public statements "in an objective and truthful manner" [10]. Likewise, the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) Code of Ethics is especially specific that computing professionals shall "avoid harm," "be honest and trustworthy," and make technological systems transparent and auditable [11]. When engineers create or endorse blockchain-based carbon systems that show non-verifiable or misleading information as sustainable progress, they violate these principles [8], [10]. Greenwashing is a moral transgression that undermines trust in the engineering profession and the emerging discipline of sustainable technology [8]. Professional accountability therefore, requires engineers to critically examine whether their systems communicate evidence-backed environmental impact [8], [9].

But evaluating responsibility for greenwashing must take account, as well, of the complexity of the engineers' working environments [8]. Engineers are rarely in charge of the whole chain of communications between technical information and public messaging [8]. Corporate sustainability departments, marketing departments, and investors can reinterpret or oversimplify technical results to meet corporate brand priorities [8]. In such a scenario, engineers can be subject to competing demands: produce novel, "green" products that win investment, but do so with scientific rigor and ethical transparency [8]. Blaming engineers alone threatens to ignore the systemic pressures that reward exaggeration and evaluate the truth as cost-ineffective [6]. Additionally, caution can hold up funds or uptake [6]. Therefore, evaluations must reconcile responsibility with reality [6]. The challenge is beyond moral clarity from single engineers, but also to design institutional safeguards [8]. This can include peer reviews of sustainability claims, transparent lifecycle analysis, and standards for independent verification [8], [9]. These all work together to protect against manipulation once the technical output leaves the lab [8].

At the structural level, the urgency for rapid decarbonization has created a market saturated with "green" narratives [6], [7]. Investors compensate climate-positive metrics; customers compensate companies that appear sustainable; policymakers maintain digital innovation in carbon tracking [6]. All these create a strong incentive to signal sustainability faster than it can be proven scientifically [6]. Blockchain initiatives that provide "traceable" and "tokenized" carbon markets thrive because they satisfy a psychological and economic need for visible responsibility [6]. Engineers working within such systems are often caught between ethical ideals and systemic forces [8]. Greenwashing will have to be addressed with institutional reform [8], [11]. It is only through aligning market incentives with ethical design that engineers can uphold their integrity in a world that commodifies environmental virtue [8], [11].
